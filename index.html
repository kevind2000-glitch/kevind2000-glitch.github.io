The inbox is crowded. The doorstep is different.

Correspondence is one of the oldest ways people build trust. A well-timed letter can reassure a customer, help a buyer make a decision, or turn a routine interaction into a relationship. Today, artificial intelligence (AI) gives organizations new ways to make those exchanges more relevant—whether the message arrives as an email, a service note, a printed postcard, a renewal reminder, or a carefully prepared package.

The opportunity is not simply to produce more messages. It is to make each message more useful. AI can help teams interpret signals, choose an appropriate moment, adapt language to a customer’s needs, and coordinate print and digital channels. But it can also generate generic copy at industrial speed, amplify inaccurate assumptions, or make a brand sound oddly intimate. The difference is the quality of the judgment around the technology.

Direct-mail research and industry guidance often emphasize the value of physical mail as a memorable, tangible part of a wider campaign. See USPS Delivers’ discussion of mail memorability and MIT Sloan Management Review’s perspective on direct mail in a digital age . Digital correspondence brings its own advantages: speed, low friction, and measurable interaction. AI can connect these strengths when teams design around the recipient rather than around the channel. Additional perspectives include JICMAIL’s study of attention to mail and WARC’s analysis of personalization in direct-mail campaigns.

The central idea: use AI to improve the relevance of a message, not to simulate a relationship that does not exist. Personalization should make correspondence feel considered, accurate, and easy to act on.

What personalization actually means

Personalization is often reduced to inserting a first name into a subject line or printing a company logo on a postcard. Those details can help identify the recipient, but they are not the same as relevance. Meaningful personalization reflects a real need, context, preference, or relationship stage.

In B2C communication, context might include a product a customer owns, the service they requested, a delivery window, or the category of content they chose to receive. In B2B correspondence, context may include an account’s industry, a business challenge publicly discussed by the organization, the recipient’s role, the stage of a sales conversation, or a known operational deadline.

AI helps translate these signals into practical choices. It can summarize account notes, group customers by needs, recommend content, draft variants, detect a likely service issue, and suggest which channel may be appropriate. The model is not the strategy; it is a tool inside a strategy. Teams still need to decide what data is appropriate, what promise the brand can make, and what action the recipient should take. For practical channel design, compare Canada Post’s campaign guide with Marketreach’s guidance on building brand trust through mail.

A BETTER ADDRESS LABEL

A useful test is to remove the recipient’s name. If the message still demonstrates an understanding of their situation, the personalization is probably doing real work. If it collapses into a generic pitch, the team may have customized the wrapper rather than the message. Personalization is a promise

Once a message uses personal data or implies knowledge about a recipient, it makes a quiet promise: this information is relevant, accurate, and being used for a good reason.
