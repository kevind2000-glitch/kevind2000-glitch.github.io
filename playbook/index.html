A practical AI-assisted workflow

The best workflow puts the recipient’s need before the model prompt. AI can accelerate research, drafting, adaptation, and analysis, but a repeatable process keeps those capabilities connected to accurate data, a clear offer, and a working response path.

  1. Define the recipient’s need. Start with the problem the communication should solve, the decision it should support, or the service task it should complete.
  2. Choose permitted signals. Use relevant first-party data and appropriate public information. Document the source, freshness, and purpose of each important field.
  3. Segment by meaningful differences. Group people by needs, lifecycle stage, or business role—not by every tiny behavior a model can detect.
  4. Generate a controlled set of variants. Ask AI for alternate openings, reading levels, calls to action, or creative concepts inside approved brand and factual boundaries.
  5. Verify before sending. Check names, addresses, product details, claims, personalization tokens, links, and accessibility. Human review should increase with risk.
  6. Connect the response path. Ensure the QR code, reply address, landing page, phone number, or email destination works and matches the promise made in the message.
  7. Measure and learn. Compare response, conversion, complaints, opt-outs, and longer-term value against a meaningful baseline or holdout group.

For direct mail, production quality matters too. Proofing, data hygiene, postal requirements, paper and format choices, and quality control all influence whether the communication reaches the right person and creates a good impression. See the USPS Office of Inspector General’s report on marketers’ barriers to direct-mail management and Printing Impressions’ guide to building a direct-mail strategy .

Measure outcomes, not just activity

AI makes it easy to produce more variants and dashboards. That can create an illusion of progress. A campaign should be judged against its purpose: qualified meetings, completed onboarding, fewer support contacts, renewal, incremental sales, or a stronger customer relationship.

Track a small set of metrics that match the job. Delivery and response rates can help diagnose reach and engagement; conversion and incremental lift can indicate whether the campaign changed behavior; cost per qualified outcome can support budget decisions. For direct mail, unique URLs, QR codes, offer codes, call tracking, and CRM campaign fields can contribute evidence. JICMAIL maintains a response-rate tracker and research into how people spend time with mail . The ANA also publishes a response-rate report .

Whenever possible, compare a personalized treatment with a sensible control group. A name-insertion test is not the same as a test of deeper relevance. Teams can compare useful content versus generic content, role-specific messaging versus one-size-fits-all copy, or coordinated mail-plus-digital sequences versus digital alone. Use consistent attribution rules and avoid claiming that a single touch caused a sale when several interactions influenced it.

Keep the evidence honest. Industry articles and vendor guides can offer examples and useful frameworks, but results depend on audience, offer, creative quality, timing, channel, and measurement method. Treat published response rates as context—not a guarantee for your campaign.

Privacy, trust, and the limits of inference

Trust requires restraint. A company does not need to mention every fact it knows about someone. Often the best personalization is quiet: the right information, at the right time, with no unnecessary explanation of how the system inferred the need.

AI systems may hallucinate facts, reproduce bias, or create language that sounds plausible but is wrong. Use approved tools, minimize data in prompts, keep access controlled, and ensure a human can intervene. For privacy guidance, consult the ICO’s explanation of legitimate interests alongside the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada . Legal obligations differ by jurisdiction and channel, so obtain appropriate review for your campaign.

A simple human-review standard

  • Lower risk: routine, factual, easily reversible messages can use approved templates and automated checks.
  • Medium risk: promotional claims, account-specific recommendations, and complex service explanations should receive targeted review.
  • Higher risk: complaints, financial hardship, legal matters, health-related communication, and other sensitive situations should have clear human ownership and escalation paths.

Before the next send

  • Can we explain the recipient benefit in one sentence?
  • Is every personalized detail accurate, current, and appropriate?
  • Does the message sound like our brand rather than generic model output?
  • Are channel preferences, opt-outs, frequency caps, and suppression rules respected?
  • Does the physical piece meet production and postal requirements?
  • Is the response path tested from envelope or inbox to final destination?
  • Have we defined a fair baseline and a metric tied to the campaign goal?
  • Can a person intervene when the situation is unusual or sensitive?
